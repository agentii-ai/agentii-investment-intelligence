# Quantitative Screening Methodology

Methodology fused from professional investment frameworks; all text is an original paraphrase.

---

## The Quantitative Process: Two-Directional Screening

Quantitative screening operates in two opposing directions simultaneously. Neither direction alone is sufficient. The combination is what produces actionable candidates.

### Direction 1: Forward-Looking Valuation (Discovery)

Scan the screening universe to answer: what is the market pricing? Where are the anomalies?

Forward-looking metrics (what the market anticipates):
- 1-year forward P/E ratio (current price / mean consensus EPS estimate for the current fiscal year)
- 2-year forward P/E ratio (current price / mean consensus EPS estimate for the next fiscal year)
- PEG ratio: forward P/E divided by forward EPS growth rate
- EV/EBITDA relative to growth rate
- Price/Sales multiple in the context of the margin profile
- Forward revenue multiples for pre-profit companies

**The core principle**: The market is mostly efficient. An outlier exists because either (a) the market is wrong (your analytical edge), or (b) you are missing something material. Your job is to determine which. Non-participation is always an option — identifying an outlier does not automatically mean initiating a position. Further work is required.

### Direction 2: Backward-Looking Financial Statement Validation (Evidence)

For every outlier identified in the forward-looking scan, validate against historical financial data. The question: do the financial statements support the market's valuation signal or contradict it?

Validation sequence (must be executed in this order):
1. Revenue quality and trajectory
2. Earnings quality and GAAP/non-GAAP reconciliation
3. Margin structure and trend
4. Cash flow conversion and sustainability

## P/E Ratio: The Professional Interpretation

### The Fundamental Misunderstanding

The P/E ratio is one of the most systematically misunderstood metrics in financial markets. The amateur interpretation — low P/E equals "cheap," high P/E equals "expensive" — is rejected at the institutional level. These words are essentially "curse words in professional trading circles."

The professional interpretation: **P/E ratio measures what the market is willing to pay for a company's forward earnings**. It is a market psychology metric — a signal of perceived earnings quality and growth sustainability — not an intrinsic value metric.

### Critical Demonstration: The Constant-Multiple Price Movement

A stock trading at 10x forward earnings can:
- Decline from $10 to $0.10 (losing 99%) while maintaining the same 10x multiple, if earnings estimates also decline proportionally
- Rise from $10 to $40 while maintaining the same 10x multiple, if earnings estimates also rise proportionally

A PEG ratio may likewise remain constant while a stock rises or falls. This is because both P/E and PEG are ratios — the numerator (price) and denominator (earnings or earnings growth) can move together, keeping the ratio constant while the stock goes anywhere.

Calling a stock "cheap" or "expensive" based on these ratios is therefore meaningless without understanding what the market is signaling about forward fundamentals.

### The Two Axioms Governing P/E-Based Analysis

1. "There is nothing you know that the market doesn't already know." The market has already processed all publicly available information into the current price and multiple.
2. "The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent." Even if you believe the market is wrong, betting against it requires precise timing — and timing is the hardest element to get right.

Implication: Identifying an outlier through P/E analysis is the START of the process, not the end. The analyst must determine WHY the market has assigned this multiple before deciding whether to agree or disagree.

### The Analytical Question

For every P/E outlier: "There must be a reason why this positive outlier trades on a premium to the sector average, and a reason why this negative outlier trades on a discount." The analyst's job is to discover that reason through financial statement validation and qualitative analysis.

## Forward-Looking Metrics: The Complete Framework

### The Four Pillars

The quantitative screening framework rests on four forward-looking pillars:
1. **Forward Earnings Growth** (EG1 = current fiscal year, EG2 = next fiscal year)
2. **Forward Revenue Growth** (RG1, RG2)
3. **Forward P/E Ratio** (PE1, PE2)
4. **Forward PEG Ratio** (PEG1 = PE1/EG1, PEG2 = PE2/EG2)

### The Analytical Sequence

**Step 1**: Filter by Forward P/E to identify outliers relative to sector median.
**Step 2**: Filter by Current Year Forward Earnings Growth (EG1) to rank outliers.
**Step 3**: Filter by Next Year Forward Earnings Growth (EG2) — the market often looks through the current year to the next.
**Step 4**: Rank by combined P/E, EG1, EG2, and Market Cap to determine the most interesting candidate.
**Step 5**: Dive into Revenue Growth to understand the relationship between top-line and bottom-line growth. Low earnings growth with high revenue growth may indicate deliberate R&D reinvestment — which is fundamentally different from operational deterioration.
**Step 6**: Calculate unit economics per share. Add back R&D per share to EPS to understand what earnings would look like if the company chose to harvest rather than invest.
**Step 7**: Calculate forward revenue multiples (Market Cap / Forward Revenue) to identify stocks where the market is applying anomalous revenue valuations.

### Earnings Growth Profile Classification

**For Long Candidates (ranked from ideal to suboptimal):**

| Profile | EG1 (Current Year) | EG2 (Next Year) | Interpretation |
|---------|:---:|:---:|------|
| 1 — Momentum | Above sector avg | More positive (accelerating) | Ideal: sequentially positive momentum |
| 2 — Solid | Above sector avg | Positive, same magnitude | Strong but not accelerating |
| 3 — Decelerating | Above sector avg | Less positive (still above avg) | Growth moderating; monitor closely |
| 4 — Sector-Average | At sector avg | At sector avg | No edge vs. simply owning the sector |
| 5 — Below-Average | Below sector avg | At or above sector avg | Potential turnaround; needs investigation |
| 6 — Weak | Below sector avg | Below sector avg | Opportunity cost to deploy capital here |

**Turnaround Profiles (long candidates, higher risk):**

| Profile | EG1 (Current Year) | EG2 (Next Year) | Interpretation |
|---------|:---:|:---:|------|
| 7 | Negative, below sector | Positive, below sector | Early turnaround signal; wait for confirmation |
| 8 | Negative, below sector | Positive, at or above sector | Genuine turnaround candidate; investigate |
| 9 | Negative, loss-making | Positive, above sector | "Opportunistic and dangerous" — highest reward, highest risk |

**For Short Candidates:**

| Profile | EG1 (Current Year) | EG2 (Next Year) | Interpretation |
|---------|:---:|:---:|------|
| A — Peak | Positive above avg, but slowing | Going flat or negative | The ex-growth tipping point. Ideal short entry zone |
| B — Decline | Negative, below avg | More negative | Trend established; ride the downgrade cycle |
| C — Guide-Down | Positive above avg | Negative (guidance cut) | Market hasn't fully priced the inflection |

### Market Cap Filtering by Direction

**Long candidates**: Concentrate on the **$3 billion to $10 billion** mid-cap space. Mid-caps offer the optimal combination of proven business models (unlike small/micro-caps with survivorship risk) and sufficient volatility for meaningful 20-60 day returns (unlike mega-caps where 6% earnings growth produces "boring" price action).

**Short candidates**: Concentrate on **$20 billion+** large and mega-cap stocks. The critical reason: shorting mid-caps exposes the trader to takeover risk. A $3 billion company receiving a 50% acquisition premium overnight creates an irrecoverable loss on a short position for small accounts. Large caps have more potential downside once they go ex-growth, and their size makes them unlikely takeover targets.

## PEG Ratio: Proper Calibration

### The Misinterpretation

Traditional academic finance teaches PEG < 1.0 as "undervalued" and PEG > 1.0 as "overvalued." This is rejected as an oversimplification that leads to systematic errors.

### The Correct Methodology

The PEG ratio must be calibrated on three dimensions:

1. **Sector-relative**: PEG must be compared to the sector median PEG, not to an absolute threshold of 1.0. A PEG of 1.5 in a sector where the median is 2.0 is relatively attractive; a PEG of 0.8 in a sector where the median is 0.5 is relatively expensive.

2. **Growth-rate-adjusted**: The relationship between P/E and growth is not linear. A 20% grower at 20x P/E (PEG = 1.0) is fundamentally different from a 5% grower at 5x P/E (PEG = 1.0). Higher growth rates command premium multiples because growth compounds; the same PEG number at different growth levels represents different investment propositions.

3. **Earnings-quality cross-checked**: PEG must be validated against earnings quality. If the "E" in PEG is inflated by aggressive accounting or non-recurring items, the PEG understates the true multiple being paid.

### Cross-Validation with EV/EBITDA-to-Growth

For asset-heavy companies where depreciation and amortization distort earnings, PEG can be misleading. Cross-validate with EV/EBITDA-to-Growth. If PEG and EV/EBITDA-to-Growth disagree materially, the depreciation policy or capital structure is distorting one of the metrics — investigate which before proceeding.

## Enterprise Value: Institutional Limitations

Standard finance education presents Enterprise Value as a superior metric to market capitalization because it accounts for debt and cash. Institutional practice recognizes four critical limitations that textbooks omit:

### Limitation 1: EV Does Not Measure Debt Quality
Enterprise Value treats all debt identically. However, a company with $5 billion of debt financing 25% ROIC projects is fundamentally different from a company with $5 billion of debt financing money-losing operations. EV tells you the debt exists; it does not tell you whether the debt is creating or destroying value.

### Limitation 2: The Market Is Not Naive
The market already knows the debt and cash positions from the most recent financial statements. There is no informational edge in calculating EV — every professional investor has the same numbers.

### Limitation 3: The Temporal Mismatch
Market capitalization is forward-looking (it discounts future cash flows). Debt and cash positions are backward-looking (they reflect the last reported balance sheet, which may be 3-6 months stale). This temporal mismatch means EV/EBIT is "very similar to P/E ratio, just with stale numbers."

### Limitation 4: EBITDA Sector Bias
EBITDA flatters capital-intensive companies (mining, oil and gas, manufacturing, infrastructure) by adding back large depreciation and amortization charges that represent real economic costs. A mining company must replace its mines; depreciation may be a non-cash charge but the capital expenditure to replace the asset is very real.

### Sector-Specific Multiple Preferences

| Sector Type | Preferred Multiple | Rationale |
|------------|:---:|------|
| Low-CAPEX (software, services, platforms) | P/E, PEG | Depreciation is minimal; earnings are a good proxy for cash flow |
| Capital-intensive (mining, energy, manufacturing) | EV/EBIT | EBIT includes depreciation (the real cost of asset replacement); EBITDA overstates earnings power |
| Highly leveraged | EV/EBITDA or EV/EBIT | EV corrects for capital structure differences that P/E ignores |
| Pre-profit / early stage | EV/Revenue or P/S | Earnings are not meaningful; revenue captures scale |
| Financial services | P/E, P/B, P/TBV | EV is not meaningful for banks (debt is part of operations, not just capital structure) |

## Peer Selection: Dual-Path Methodology

Peer selection is the foundation of relative valuation. Incorrect peer groups produce meaningless multiples. The institutional approach uses two independent paths that must converge.

### Path 1: Sector Classification
1. Start with GICS sub-industry or NAICS classification
2. Identify direct competitors from the company's 10-K "Competition" section
3. Cross-reference with sell-side equity research coverage universes
4. Check merger documents, fairness opinions, and proxy statements — these often contain the most carefully constructed peer groups because they face legal scrutiny

### Path 2: Fundamental Clustering
1. Cluster by growth rate (revenue growth, EPS growth)
2. Cluster by margin profile (gross margin, EBITDA margin)
3. Cluster by ROIC and capital intensity
4. Cluster by market cap and liquidity

### Convergence Check

Both paths should produce largely overlapping peer sets. If they diverge, one of two things is happening:
- The company is misclassified by GICS/NAICS (common: conglomerates, companies in transition)
- The company's fundamentals are so different from its nominal peers that standard sector comparisons are misleading

Either case requires investigation. Never proceed with peer-based valuation when the two paths disagree.

### Peer Group Construction Rules
- 4-6 comparable companies is the institutional standard
- Too few (< 4): insufficient statistical meaning
- Too many (> 8): dilution of comparability; lazy inclusion
- Exclude the target from peer group statistics; the target is what you are comparing AGAINST the peers
- Use median, not mean, for peer group central tendency (mean is skewed by outliers)

## Financial Statement Validation: The Evidence Sequence

For every outlier candidate, validate against backward-looking financial data in this specific order:

### 1. Revenue Analysis
- **Growth trajectory**: Accelerating, steady, or decelerating? Use 3-5 year CAGR with annual breakdown
- **Revenue quality**: Organic (same-store, volume-driven, price-driven) vs. acquisition-driven vs. one-time (license sales, asset sales)
- **Concentration risk**: Customer concentration (> 10% from single customer is material), product concentration, geographic concentration
- **Deferred revenue / RPO trends**: For subscription businesses, changes in deferred revenue or remaining performance obligations often lead recognized revenue

### 2. Earnings Quality
- **GAAP vs. non-GAAP reconciliation**: Large and persistent gaps require investigation. If non-GAAP profits consistently exceed GAAP profits by > 20%, the adjustments must be justified or the earnings are overstated.
- **SBC assessment**: Share-based compensation exceeding 10% of revenue indicates significant shareholder dilution being excluded from non-GAAP metrics
- **Non-recurring items**: Catalog every "non-recurring" charge over the last 8 quarters. If they appear in 3+ of the last 4 quarters, they are recurring by definition
- **Tax rate normalization**: Unusually low tax rates inflate earnings; normalize to statutory rate for comparison

### 3. Margin Analysis
- **Gross margin trend**: Expanding (pricing power, scale economies) or contracting (competitive pressure, input cost inflation)?
- **Operating margin**: Is the company demonstrating operating leverage (revenue growing faster than operating expenses)?
- **Incremental margins**: For each additional dollar of revenue, what percentage flows to operating profit? Above 50% indicates strong operating leverage; below 20% indicates cost growth outpacing revenue
- **R&D and S&M efficiency**: Is R&D spend producing revenue growth? Is S&M spend producing customer acquisition? Calculate revenue per dollar of S&M.

### 4. Cash Flow Analysis
- **FCF Conversion**: Free Cash Flow / Net Income. This is the ultimate earnings quality test.
  - Above 80%: excellent quality; earnings are backed by cash
  - 70-80%: acceptable; investigate any gap
  - 50-70%: concerning; requires specific explanation
  - Below 50%: hard stop for long candidates. Earnings are not converting to cash.
- **Working Capital Efficiency**: DSO (accounts receivable collection), DIO (inventory management), DPO (supplier financing). Simultaneous DSO expansion + inventory build = potential channel stuffing or demand slowdown.
- **Capital Intensity**: Capex / Revenue ratio. Rising capital intensity means more investment required to maintain growth — future FCF will be lower than current FCF suggests.

## Growth Profile Assessment

For long candidates, assess the earnings growth trajectory beyond just the growth rate:

### 1. Historical EPS Trend
Calculate 3-5 year CAGR. Consistency of growth matters more than magnitude. A company growing EPS at 12% annually for 5 consecutive years is higher quality than one growing at 18% with high year-to-year variance.

### 2. Consensus Estimates Trajectory
The direction of analyst estimate revisions is often more informative than the absolute estimate level:
- **Rising estimates + rising price**: Momentum is aligned; the market is rewarding improving fundamentals
- **Rising estimates + flat price**: Potential opportunity; the market hasn't fully priced the improvement
- **Falling estimates + rising price**: Danger signal. The market is fighting the fundamental signal. This pattern often precedes sharp reversals
- **Falling estimates + falling price**: Momentum is aligned downward; fundamentals deteriorating as expected

### 3. Earnings Surprise History
Calculate beat frequency and magnitude over the last 8-12 quarters:
- **Beat frequency > 70%**: management consistently guides conservatively; actual earnings power is higher than guidance suggests
- **Beat frequency < 30%**: management either lacks visibility or systematically overpromises
- **The "beat and raise" pattern** (beat estimates + raise guidance) is the strongest positive signal. It indicates management has both visibility into the business and confidence in the trajectory.
- **The "beat but no raise" pattern** (beat estimates + maintain guidance) is ambiguous. It may indicate conservative guidance, or it may indicate that the beat came from non-recurring items that management knows won't repeat.

### 4. Growth Quality
Is EPS growth driven by revenue growth (sustainable) or cost-cutting and buybacks (finite)?
- Revenue-driven growth: top-line expansion → operating leverage → EPS growth. Sustainable as long as revenue grows.
- Cost-cutting-driven growth: flat revenue + falling costs → EPS growth. Finite — costs can only be cut so far.
- Buyback-driven growth: flat net income + shrinking share count → EPS growth. Financial engineering, not business improvement.

## Turnaround vs. Value Trap: Financial Scorecard

Turnarounds offer the highest potential returns but carry the highest risk of being value traps. The distinction between the two is quantitative before it is qualitative.

### Turnaround Financial Indicators (weighted, scored 0-10)

| Indicator | Target Range | Weight | Turnaround Signal | Value Trap Signal |
|-----------|:---:|:---:|------|------|
| Debt / EBITDA | < 4x | 20% | Below 4x and improving | Above 6x or rising |
| Interest Coverage (EBIT / Interest) | > 3x | 20% | Above 3x, stable or improving | Below 1.5x |
| FCF Generation | Positive | 20% | Positive 2+ consecutive quarters | Persistently negative |
| Gross Margin Trend | Stable or expanding | 15% | Stabilized after declining, now flat or up | Continuing to contract |
| Industry Trajectory | Cyclical | 15% | Cyclical downturn; demand will return | Structural decline; demand permanently shifted |
| Management | New, track record | 10% | New team with prior turnaround success | Same team, repeatedly missed recovery timelines |

Composite score interpretation:
- 7-10: Strong turnaround candidate; proceed to qualitative analysis
- 4-6: Ambiguous; requires more data or a specific catalyst to become actionable
- 0-3: Probable value trap; avoid or use as short candidate

### Key Distinction: Cyclical vs. Structural Impairment
- **Cyclical**: Demand temporarily depressed by macro factors. Industry has historically recovered. Competitors similarly affected.
- **Structural**: Demand permanently shifted by technology, regulation, or consumer behavior. Industry in secular decline. Company losing to competitors with superior business models.

If the impairment is structural, no amount of management change or cost-cutting will restore the business. The stock is cheap for a reason.

### Exclude Revenue Growth Stories from Turnaround Processing
Management may deliberately reinvest earnings into top-line growth (R&D, S&M), creating suppressed current EPS. These companies are not genuine turnarounds — they are growth companies choosing to invest. Before classifying any company as a turnaround, verify that low current earnings are not the result of deliberate reinvestment by checking revenue growth and R&D/S&M spend trends.

## Sector Cleaning: The Proprietary Edge

The highest-quality quantitative screening requires building your own sector data. Third-party data dumps contain systematic errors that corrupt screening results. The professional process:

### The Seven-Step Sector Cleaning Protocol

**Step 1 — Download and Make Live**: Download raw data for the target sector. Make all cells formula-driven (never "paste special" values). As long as stock price and EPS estimates are accurate, every derived metric (EG, P/E, PEG) can be calculated via formulas.

**Step 2 — Eyeball Every Number**: Do not trust third-party data. Scan every cell for obvious errors before running any screens.

**Step 3 — Fix Positive-to-Negative EPS Transitions**: When a company goes from profit to loss (or vice versa), automated earnings growth calculations produce nonsensical results (e.g., -400% decline). A company can only lose 100% of its earnings. Manual override: +100% for loss-to-profit, -100% for profit-to-loss.

**Step 4 — Check for Law-of-Small-Numbers Distortion**: Earnings growth from a tiny EPS base ($0.01 to $0.10 = 900%) creates wild numbers that distort sector averages. Look at the last 2-5 years of EPS history. A company showing 900% growth from $0.01 to $0.10 EPS may have earned $0.12 two years ago — making it the worst stock in the sector despite the highest growth rate.

**Step 5 — Align Fiscal Year Ends**: Within a single GICS/NAICS sector, companies may have different fiscal year ends (January 31, March 31, June 30, December 31). Third-party data may place the wrong year's EPS in the wrong column. Check fiscal year-end dates and manually align.

**Step 6 — Check Contributions to the Mean**: Two stocks with extreme values can contribute 75% of a sector's average earnings growth. Calculate sector averages with and without extreme outliers to identify distortion. If removal changes the average by > 30%, the outliers are dominating and the unadjusted average is misleading.

**Step 7 — Build Your Own Classification**: GICS and NAICS classifications are not your classifications. Companies may be misclassified (conglomerates), sectors may contain fundamentally incomparable businesses (compare $200B mega-caps to $1B small-caps within the same NAICS code), and growth rate differentials within a sector may be so large that comparisons are meaningless. Split sectors by market cap if necessary. Remove stocks that do not belong.

### The Working-Smart Constraint
Never attempt to clean data for all 2,200+ stocks in the screening universe. This is "working too hard, not working smart." Only perform the full cleaning protocol on stocks that have passed the initial screen (forward P/E outlier detection) — typically fewer than 20 candidates.

## Data Mining Bias Prevention

Quantitative screening is vulnerable to systematic biases that produce false signals. The institutional analyst actively guards against each of these:

### Survivorship Bias
Screening only current index constituents ignores companies that were delisted, bankrupted, or acquired during the backtest period. Any backtest using today's universe on historical data is contaminated — the worst outcomes have been removed from the dataset.

**Mitigation**: Use point-in-time index constituents for historical backtests. Accept that perfectly clean historical data may be unavailable and flag the limitation.

### Look-Ahead Bias
Using information in a backtest that would not have been available at the time of the decision. Most commonly: using restated financials in historical screening when the restatement occurred later; using current analyst estimates to screen historical periods; using current sector classifications for historical companies.

**Mitigation**: Use original-as-reported financial data for historical periods. Flag when only restated data is available.

### The Multiple Testing Problem (Data Snooping)
Testing 100 screening parameters at 95% confidence produces approximately 5 "statistically significant" results by random chance alone. Without out-of-sample validation, these spurious correlations appear to be genuine edges.

**Mitigation**: Three-part defense — (1) out-of-sample holdout testing, (2) preference for simple rules over complex ones (fewer parameters = fewer degrees of freedom = less overfitting risk), (3) economic rationale requirement (every screening parameter must have a logical economic explanation for why it should work; purely empirical patterns are suspect).

### Sector-Neutral Comparison Error
Comparing valuation multiples across sectors without adjusting for sector norms. A technology company at 25x P/E may be inexpensive; a utility at 25x P/E is expensive. Sector-specific capital intensity, growth rates, and risk profiles create legitimate multiple differences.

**Mitigation**: Always use sector-relative comparisons. The question is never "is 15x P/E cheap?" but "is 15x P/E cheap relative to the sector median, given this company's growth rate and margin profile?"

### The Law of Small Numbers (EPS Base Effect)
Earnings growth percentages from tiny EPS bases produce extreme values (500%, 1,000%, 5,000%) that are statistical noise, not analytical signals. A company going from $0.01 to $0.10 EPS shows 900% growth, but the absolute earnings remain negligible.

**Mitigation**: Always display absolute EPS alongside percentage growth. Flag any EG value derived from an EPS base below $0.25 as potentially distorted by the law of small numbers.

## Market Cap Filtering by Strategy Type

Market capitalization is not just a size filter — it determines which strategies are viable:

### Long Candidates
- **Mid-Cap ($3B-$10B)**: The institutional sweet spot. Proven business models (unlike small caps), sufficient volatility for meaningful returns (unlike mega caps), and enough analyst coverage for reliable consensus estimates.
- **Small-Cap ($1B-$3B)**: Higher risk. Thin analyst coverage means consensus estimates may be stale or unreliable. Higher volatility but also higher failure rate.
- **Mega-Cap ($40B+)**: Generally avoided for long trades in the 20-60 day horizon. Low earnings growth translates to low volatility, producing "boring" price action that doesn't generate sufficient returns for active trading.

### Short Candidates
- **Large-Cap ($20B+)**: The institutional minimum for short selling. Large caps have more room to fall once they go ex-growth, and their size makes them unlikely acquisition targets.
- **Mid-Cap ($3B-$10B)**: **Avoid for shorts.** A 50% acquisition premium on a mid-cap short creates an irrecoverable loss for small accounts. Takeover risk is real and asymmetric: your downside is limited to 100% on a long, but your downside on a short is theoretically infinite.
- **Small/Micro-Cap (< $3B)**: Never short. Takeover risk, short squeeze risk, and liquidity risk are all elevated.

## The Short Side: Ex-Growth Analysis

### The Five-Phase Ex-Growth Decline Template

When a growth stock loses its growth premium, the decline follows a predictable pattern:

| Phase | Description | Price Action | Trader Action |
|:---:|------|------|------|
| 1 | Peak — Growth capacity reached, multiple at maximum, bullish hubris, management reluctant to lower guidance | Range-bound at highs | Monitor for Phase 2 transition signals |
| 2 | Expectations shift — Lower guidance issued (reluctantly), top-slicing begins, stock moves BEFORE the official announcement | First leg down, often sharp | **Ideal short entry** |
| 3 | New normal established — Stale longs flushed out, turnaround talk begins, DCA investors enter | Second leg down, grinding | Short entry still viable |
| 4 | Deep decline — Stock down 50-90% from highs, new MOP rolled out, sideways with ugly squeezes | Volatile, range-bound | **Do not short**. Value investors stepping in create squeeze risk |
| 5 | Resolution — Turnaround succeeds or fails | Directional depending on outcome | Irrelevant for 20-60 day trading |

**The trading window is Phase 2 and Phase 3**. Shorting in Phase 4 or 5 means fighting both value investors and potential turnaround momentum — a low-probability proposition.

### Short Interest as a Positioning Check

- **Short Interest %** = Shares Sold Short / Public Float (sentiment gauge)
- **Days to Cover** = Shares Sold Short / Average Daily Volume (liquidity gauge)

Institutional thresholds for S&P 500 stocks:
- Days to Cover < 10: normal positioning; no squeeze risk
- Days to Cover 10-20: elevated short interest; monitor
- Days to Cover > 20: dangerous; avoid — you are joining a crowded trade
- Days to Cover > 30: extreme — the squeeze risk dominates any fundamental thesis

Avoid "zero to hero" shorts: consensus short positions that could violently reverse on any positive news.

### Dividend-Paying Stocks as Shorts
When short a dividend-paying stock, the short seller owes the dividend payment to the stock lender. The stock price adjusts downward by the dividend amount on the ex-dividend date, but automatic dividend reinvestment by long shareholders typically pushes the price higher than the adjustment price. Shorting dividend payers requires conviction that the dividend will be cut — otherwise the quarterly dividend obligation erodes returns.
