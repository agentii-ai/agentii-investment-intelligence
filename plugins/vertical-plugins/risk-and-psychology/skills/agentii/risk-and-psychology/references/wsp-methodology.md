# Corporate Restructuring — Institutional Methodology

Advanced restructuring process methodology synthesized from institutional training; all text is original paraphrase. No basic definitions. Practitioner-grade operational knowledge only.

---

## Out-of-Court vs. In-Court Decision Framework

The six-dimensional comparison where advantages flip by category:

**Out-of-Court advantages**: (1) **Cost** — Chapter 11 legal fees drain the estate of critical cash when it is scarcest. (2) **Speed** — out-of-court is significantly faster, though prepackaged bankruptcies increasingly compress the Chapter 11 timeline. (3) **Business disruption** — out-of-court remains private; in-court exposes all creditor claims, asset schedules, and recoveries publicly.

**In-Court advantages**: (1) **Automatic stay** — immediately upon filing, all creditor collection, eviction, and litigation cease. This is the single most decisive factor for many filers. (2) **Holdout resolution** — the confirmed plan binds the entire class with no opt-out, solving the holdout problem that plagues out-of-court workouts. (3) **DIP financing** — debtor-in-possession lenders receive super-priority over existing secured creditors, making post-petition lending viable when no rational lender would extend credit out-of-court. (4) **Contract rejection** — unfavorable leases, pension obligations, and executory contracts can be rejected. (5) **Tax** — cancellation of debt income is deductible in bankruptcy but taxable out-of-court. (6) **363 sales** — assets sold free and clear of all liens, avoiding fraudulent-transfer risk that attaches to out-of-court distressed sales.

**Operating principle**: Out-of-court restructuring is driven entirely by the bankruptcy framework. Creditors only agree if they believe they cannot do materially better in court. The bankruptcy waterfall is the BATNA shaping every negotiation.

## Chapter 11 Operational Timeline

**Pre-filing cash management**: Debtors actively manage timing — delaying vendor payments, maxing out revolving credit lines, and filing immediately before a large required principal repayment. These practices are standard (short of fraud).

**Filing**: The debtor files a voluntary Chapter 11 petition. The debtor nearly always files preemptively — even when creditors could initiate an involuntary petition — because the filer selects the jurisdiction, a significant strategic advantage.

**Automatic stay effective immediately**. Post-petition liabilities receive priority treatment over pre-petition claims, enabling continued operations with vendors who now have administrative claim status.

**Management continuity**: Existing management remains post-petition barring fraud or gross mismanagement. However, fiduciary duty shifts from equity to creditors once the company enters the "zone of insolvency" — a fact-specific determination that is difficult to pinpoint.

**DIP financing negotiation**: DIP lenders negotiate super-priority liens ahead of existing secured creditors. DIP agreements typically include "carve-outs" for professional fees — although legal and advisory fees are technically unsecured administrative claims, the DIP lender consents to setting aside a portion of collateral for these payments to ensure the restructuring process can function.

## Recovery Waterfall & The Absolute Priority Rule

| Priority | Claim Class | Operational Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Super-priority secured (DIP) | Post-petition lenders; above all pre-petition secured |
| 2 | Secured pre-petition | Bifurcated: secured to collateral value, deficiency becomes GUC |
| 3 | Administrative | Professional fees, post-petition operating expenses |
| 4 | Priority unsecured | Accrued employee wages, statutory priorities |
| 5 | General unsecured (GUCs) | Trade creditors, unsecured lenders, deficiency claims |
| 6 | Equity | Typically wiped out; may receive nominal "tip" to prevent obstruction |

**The absolute priority rule**: No junior class receives anything until all senior classes are paid in full. In practice, this is violated constantly — unsecured creditors and equity often receive recovery even when senior classes are impaired, because underwater claimholders can threaten litigation and reduce overall recoveries unless negotiated with. The gap between statutory priority and actual distribution is where restructuring advisory value is concentrated.

**Undersecured claim bifurcation**: A $100M claim secured by collateral appraised at $80M splits into an $80M secured claim and a $20M general unsecured claim. Second-lien holders are affected disproportionately — if collateral covers only the first lien, the entire second lien becomes unsecured.

**Collateral valuation disputes**: The second lien argues for higher collateral value (enlarging its secured claim at GUC expense). GUCs argue for lower value (pushing more second-lien exposure into the unsecured pool for pro rata sharing). This single variable can swing aggregate recoveries significantly — a $200K to $240K increase in assumed collateral value shifted second-lien aggregate recovery from 55.3% to 68.3%.

## Fulcrum Security Identification

The fulcrum security is the most junior claim class still receiving partial recovery — where enterprise value "runs out." Below the fulcrum, claims are wiped out; at and above, recoveries are positive. The fulcrum class typically receives the equity in the reorganized entity.

**Trading-based identification**: Compare claim trading prices against estimated recoveries. Second-lien trading at 50 with projected 55-69% recovery suggests mispricing — but the equity-heavy form of recovery creates uncertainty. Unsecured notes trading at 20 with a 40-54% projected recovery represent the classic distressed opportunity: high upside with a defined bankruptcy floor.

**Structural subordination factors that override statutory priority**: (1) Opco vs. holdco debt placement — debt at the operating company level has de facto priority. (2) Inter-creditor subordination agreements — side deals that subordinate one lender to another without affecting other creditors. (3) Maturity timing — out-of-court, a lower-priority loan coming due may receive full payment while higher-priority longer-dated claims get partial. (4) Perfection challenges — secured status can be contested if documentation is incomplete. (5) Equitable subordination — courts can equalize claims under certain circumstances.

## Restructuring Plan Negotiation Dynamics

**Stakeholder BATNA analysis by class**:

- **First lien (fully covered by collateral)**: Will reject any haircut because they can expect 100% recovery in court. Their agreement is only obtainable if their claim is left intact.
- **Second lien (bifurcated)**: Accepts blended recovery of reduced debt plus majority equity — often becoming the controlling shareholder. Aggregate recovery of 55.3% (when trading at 50% of par) is acceptable because it beats the market's expectation and the second lien is the only class comfortable holding equity.
- **Unsecured notes (deeply discounted in market)**: A 40% recovery against 20% of par trading price looks attractive, even if it appears poor in absolute terms.
- **Trade creditors (non-lenders)**: Most likely to be dissatisfied. They may demand COD terms or refuse future supply, giving them operational leverage beyond claim size.
- **Original equity**: Cannot be unilaterally wiped out out-of-court. Standard compromise: 5% retention taken from GUC recoveries in exchange for not obstructing.

**The central tension**: The plan must balance two opposing forces — reducing leverage sufficiently for the business to emerge as a viable going concern while providing enough recovery to each class that the bankruptcy alternative is not preferable. Increasing post-restructuring debt tolerance improves recoveries but raises re-default probability.

**Valuation as the pivot**: The entire framework depends on enterprise valuation — how large the "pie" is determines what each class receives. Yet there is no firm guidance on how to value a distressed estate. DCF, multiples, and liquidation analysis are all deployed, and each stakeholder argues for the methodology favoring their recovery. The most aggressive fights occur around collateral value and enterprise value — not around the priority rules themselves.
