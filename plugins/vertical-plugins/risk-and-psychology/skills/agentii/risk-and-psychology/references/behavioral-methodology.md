# Behavioral Decision Framework — Institutional Methodology

Methodology synthesized from institutional investment research; all text is an original paraphrase.

---

## Good Losses vs. Bad Losses

All losses are not created equal. The institutional framework distinguishes between two fundamentally different types of losses:

### Good Loss: Sound Process, Adverse Outcome

A good loss occurs when the decision process was correct but the outcome was unfavorable. The thesis was well-formed, the catalyst was identifiable, position sizing was appropriate, and the entry followed the declared strategy. The loss resulted from factors outside the analyst's control or from the inherent probability distribution (a 60% probability trade will lose 40% of the time).

**Response**: Maintain the strategy. The process is working as designed. A good loss is the cost of doing business in probabilistic environments.

### Bad Loss: Flawed Process

A bad loss occurs when the decision process was flawed, regardless of outcome. The thesis was incomplete (missing the catalyst or variant view), position sizing violated risk limits, or the entry was driven by emotion rather than analysis.

**Response**: Fix the process, not the P&L. A bad loss is a signal that the analytical or execution framework needs improvement.

### The "Bad Win" (Most Dangerous Outcome)

A bad win occurs when a flawed process produces a favorable outcome. The stock went up, but for reasons unrelated to the thesis. The catalyst never materialized but a macro tailwind or sector rotation carried the stock higher.

Bad wins are the most dangerous outcomes because they reinforce flawed behavior. The analyst concludes "the process worked" when in fact luck was favorable. Bad wins are the primary source of overconfidence and subsequent large losses.

**Response**: Classify as a process failure despite the positive P&L. Do not increase conviction in the strategy. Do not increase position sizes based on this outcome.

## Process-Quality Checklist (Independent of P&L)

After every trade (win or loss), answer these three questions before looking at the P&L:

### 1. Entry Quality
- [ ] Was the thesis documented BEFORE entry (not rationalized afterward)?
- [ ] Did the thesis contain all five required elements (consensus, variant, catalyst, return, risk)?
- [ ] Was the catalyst identifiable, dateable, and within the declared time horizon?
- [ ] Was the entry consistent with the declared strategy (not a deviation rationalized as "opportunistic")?

### 2. Sizing Quality
- [ ] Was position size within declared risk limits (default 3-5%)?
- [ ] Was the sizing adjusted for catalyst type (binary → reduced, spectrum → standard)?
- [ ] Was the sizing adjusted for conviction level?
- [ ] Was correlation with existing positions checked and accounted for?

### 3. Exit Quality
- [ ] Was the exit triggered by the declared catalyst resolution (or failure)?
- [ ] Was the exit at the declared stop level or target (not an emotional intra-trade decision)?
- [ ] Was the exit documented with the same rigor as the entry?

**Scoring**:
- 3/3 yes on all three dimensions + positive P&L = Good Win (reinforce strategy)
- 3/3 yes on all three dimensions + negative P&L = Good Loss (maintain strategy)
- < 3/3 yes on any dimension = Process Failure (fix the process)
- < 3/3 yes + positive P&L = BAD WIN (most dangerous — do not increase conviction)

## Bayesian Decision Framework

### The Core Principle

All investment decisions should be framed as Bayesian updates: start with a prior probability based on base rates (the outside view), then update with new, case-specific evidence to arrive at a posterior probability.

```
Posterior Probability ∝ Prior (Base Rate) × Likelihood (New Evidence)
```

### Step 1: Establish the Base Rate (Outside View)

Before analyzing the specific company, determine the reference class base rate:
- What percentage of companies in this industry sustain ROIC > WACC for 5+ years?
- What percentage of turnarounds in this sector succeed?
- What percentage of companies with this growth rate maintain it for 3+ years?
- What percentage of management teams with this profile deliver on guidance?

The base rate is the anchor. It prevents the most common analytical error: believing the specific case is an exception before establishing that exceptions exist.

### Step 2: Gather New Evidence (Likelihood)

For each piece of case-specific evidence, ask: if the thesis is correct, how likely would I be to observe this evidence? If the thesis is incorrect, how likely would I be to observe it?

Evidence that is equally likely under both hypotheses provides no diagnostic value. Evidence that is MUCH more likely under one hypothesis than the other is high-diagnostic-value.

### Step 3: Update and Calibrate

```
Posterior Odds = Prior Odds × Likelihood Ratio
```

Where Likelihood Ratio = P(Evidence | Thesis True) / P(Evidence | Thesis False)

### Step 4: Flag Structural Breaks

Base rates are not stationary. When the underlying data-generating process changes (e.g., the rise of intangible-intensive business models), historical base rates become less reliable. Flag when:
- Industry structure has fundamentally changed (deregulation, technology disruption)
- The company's business model has no historical analogue
- The reference class is too small for reliable base rates (< 30 observations)

## Base Rate Neglect

The most common and costly behavioral error in investment analysis is base rate neglect — the tendency to ignore the statistical distribution of outcomes in the reference class and focus exclusively on case-specific information.

### Examples of Base Rate Neglect

- "This turnaround will succeed because the new CEO has a great track record." (Base rate: ~30% of turnarounds succeed regardless of CEO quality.)
- "This company will sustain 25% revenue growth because the TAM is enormous." (Base rate: < 5% of companies sustain > 20% revenue growth for 5+ years.)
- "This stock is cheap at 8× earnings." (Base rate: cheap stocks can stay cheap or get cheaper. Valuation alone does not predict returns.)

### Mitigation
1. Always start with the base rate before analyzing the specific case.
2. If the base rate is unfavorable, the analytical burden of proof is HIGHER, not lower.
3. Document the base rate explicitly in the thesis. If the thesis contradicts the base rate, explain why THIS case is different.

## Outcome Bias

Outcome bias is the tendency to judge a decision by its outcome rather than by the quality of the decision process. In investing, where randomness plays a significant role in short-term outcomes, outcome bias is the primary obstacle to learning.

### The Fix: Process-First Post-Mortem
1. Evaluate the DECISION PROCESS first (checklist above).
2. Only after process evaluation, look at the P&L.
3. If process was sound and P&L is negative → Good Loss. Maintain strategy.
4. If process was flawed and P&L is positive → Bad Win. Fix strategy despite the profit.

A portfolio manager who evaluates analysts by P&L alone will incentivize risk-taking over skill. A manager who evaluates by process quality will develop genuine analytical edge over time.
