# Long/Short Equity — Institutional Methodology

## The Consensus Disconnect Framework

The central analytical discipline in long/short investing is identifying a **variant view** — an earnings or valuation estimate that diverges materially from market consensus. Consensus, however, is not a single number. Sell-side consensus (the Bloomberg average) is typically stale: it updates only around earnings releases, leaving the stock price free to incorporate changing market views in the interim. The buy side moves ahead of the sell side.

Developing a buy-side consensus estimate follows a deliberate process: (1) talk to contacts who are also invested in the name to surface their assumptions and pressure points, (2) talk to those on the opposite side of the trade — longs must understand the short case and vice versa, and (3) synthesize these inputs into a best-guess buy-side estimate. The true consensus against which to measure a variant view is where the buy side sits, not the Bloomberg number. This is inherently imprecise, which is why rigor in the research process becomes the differentiator.

## Idea Sourcing and Filtering

Institutional idea generation operates across five channels, used in combination:

1. **Industry research and trade shows**: Attending sector-specific events (CES, industry conferences) provides ground-level intelligence on competitive dynamics, pricing trends, and emerging threats that financial statements have not yet reflected.
2. **Tangential analysis**: Investigating a company in one sector frequently surfaces more compelling opportunities in adjacent industries. A thesis on railroads may lead to a thesis on rail car manufacturers. This cascading exploration is a primary source of differentiated ideas.
3. **Conversations with analysts and industry participants**: Executives within an industry hold strong, informed views on competitive positioning. These conversations surface non-consensus perspectives that are not in sell-side reports.
4. **Idea platforms**: Forums such as SumZero and Value Investors Club host detailed, thesis-driven write-ups from professional investors. These serve as initial leads requiring independent verification.
5. **Stock screens and proprietary filters**: Beyond standard screens (low P/E, 52-week lows, high/low ROIC), funds employ custom quantitative filters calibrated to their specific strategy parameters.

## The Three-Phase Investment Process

Research follows a structured pipeline with explicit time gates:

**Phase 1 — Getting Up to Speed (1 day to 1 week):** Read annual and quarterly filings. Review earnings call transcripts for management tone, analyst pressure points, and communication style. Study initiation-of-coverage reports from brokers — these contain industry background, long-term trend data, and company history that provide the "gears" of the business model. The goal is not detailed modeling but contextual fluency.

**Phase 2 — Answer the Root Questions (1 week to 3 months):** Limit analysis to three to five factors that genuinely drive the outcome. Beyond five, the analyst is spread too thin. Use historical case studies to pressure-test the current thesis — have similar challenges arisen before, and what happened? Assess competitive advantage sustainability through benchmarking against peers. Evaluate the expansion opportunity through per-capita store penetration, competitor footprint analysis, or addressable market sizing. A healthy competitive benchmark looks like: three peers performing well, one underperforming — the gap represents the recoverable opportunity.

**Phase 3 — Assess Risk/Reward (1 to 4 weeks):** Construct a quarterly operating model for the near term and annual projections for the long term. Frame the risk/reward through three cases (bear, base, bull) using either earnings-multiple or DCF-based valuation. The asymmetry must be explicit: materially more upside than downside. Timing is critical because IRR is sensitive to the realization period — 30% upside over five years is a materially different investment than 30% over one year. Synthesize findings into a memo or presentation that leads with three to five thesis points, supported by the full analytical work.

## Long Strategy Archetypes

Four distinct long strategies sit within the earnings disconnect framework, each with its own process requirements:

- **Event-Driven Turnaround**: Benchmark the company against competitors. The ideal: peers are performing well while this company has been poorly managed. Assess whether management has the track record and alignment to execute the recovery. Post-entry, the role shifts to monitoring execution against stated plans.
- **Activist**: Same initial benchmarking, but the analyst is the catalyst. Before taking a stake, determine procedural viability: is there a controlling shareholder, multiple share classes, a staggered board, or bylaw provisions that block influence? Only then engage management — in person or via regulatory filing.
- **Value/Deep Value**: Determine the net present value of future cash flows, whether from ongoing operations or from liquidation/sale of assets. Catalysts are debated: some funds require a specific event to surface value; others trust the market to eventually price it.
- **Growth/GARP**: Assess whether the competitive advantage is genuine and durable. Determine the NPV of cash flows incorporating the true growth trajectory. The key risk is overpaying for growth that proves unsustainable.

## The L/S Strategy Spectrum

Strategies exist on a continuum from profit/spread-focused to fundamentals-focused:

| Strategy | Time Horizon | Orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Merger Arbitrage | 0–18 months | Spread-focused |
| General Event-Driven | 0–24 months | Spread-leaning |
| Activist | 0–24 months | Intermediate |
| Value/Deep Value | 0–5+ years | Fundamentals |
| Growth/GARP | 0–5+ years | Fundamentals |

These are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive. Every fund operates with a unique style that blends elements across categories.

## Short Idea Generation

Three archetypes for short candidates, each with distinct risk profiles:

1. **Competition shorts**: A new, structurally superior competitor is displacing an incumbent. The short thesis requires evidence that the competitive moat is being breached irreversibly.
2. **Consumer euphoria shorts**: A fad product experiences parabolic demand followed by collapse. Timing is paramount — the euphoria phase can persist longer than fundamentals warrant.
3. **Disappearing business shorts (secular decliners)**: Counterintuitively, these are among the hardest shorts to execute. Slow-declining businesses often generate substantial cash flow, which management redeploys into acquisitions and adjacent expansion, prolonging the decline and frustrating short sellers.

## Alpha Shorting vs. Index Shorting

These are mechanically distinct activities serving different purposes. Index shorting (shorting the S&P 500 ETF) hedges the long book's beta — operationally simple, contributes zero selection alpha. Alpha shorting takes individual short positions expected to generate profit in their own right. It is far more valued by investors because it is far harder to execute. Using alpha shorts as a beta hedge incurs the analytical cost of the former with the return profile of the latter.

## Short-Side Structural Constraints

The short side is not a mirror of the long side. Seven constraints demand explicit accounting:

1. **Long bias of equity markets**: The unconditional drift is upward, creating a persistent headwind.
2. **Unbounded loss**: Gains cap at 100%; losses are theoretically unlimited.
3. **Shorter time horizon**: The market enforces less patience on shorts.
4. **Borrow availability and cost**: Shares must be locatable and remain locatable. Borrow fees (e.g., 3% annually) and dividend payments to the lender create a negative carry that compounds over time.
5. **Short interest and days-to-cover**: Crowded shorts (high short interest, high days-to-cover) invite squeezes. A positive surprise triggers simultaneous covering and dramatic upward price moves.
6. **Volatility asymmetry**: A losing short grows into the book as it moves against the position, automatically increasing the exposure you least want — the opposite dynamic of a long, which shrinks its weight when it falls.
7. **Constrained sizing**: Because of (2) and (6), shorts require smaller initial positions and firmer stops. A book with 20 long positions may carry 40 short positions, each individually smaller.

## Mosaic Construction

The analyst does not seek a single definitive data point. The objective is triangulation: gathering multiple independent indicators that converge on the same answer. Per-capita store penetration in one state, competitor footprint in another, management's historical execution track record, and industry trend data each contribute one tile to a mosaic that collectively supports the investment thesis.
